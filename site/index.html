The shortest walk that covers every street

A postman has to walk down every street of a district and return to the office. Some streets must be walked twice; the question is which ones, so that the extra distance is as small as possible.

This is the Chinese Postman Problem, the subject of a final-year engineering project in 1997. The 2026 revision works on two-way streets, one-way streets and towns that mix them, and on streets that cost more in one direction. It also handles districts where only some streets need service, and work shared among several postmen or vehicles with a limited capacity.

The problem

A route that walks every street exactly once and returns to its start exists only when every junction joins an even number of streets: Euler proved this for the seven bridges of Königsberg, in a paper written in 1735 and published in 1741. Real districts have junctions with an odd number of streets, so some streets must be walked again. The Chinese mathematician Kwan Mei-Ko posed the question in 1960: which streets should be repeated so that the extra distance is as small as possible?

The answer pairs up the odd junctions so that the total distance between paired junctions is minimal, repeats the streets along those connections, and then walks the result. Mail delivery, refuse collection, snow ploughing and street sweeping all build on this problem, usually with added limits such as vehicle capacity or working shifts.

The 2026 revision at a glance

40,000
junctions in the largest synthetic towns. All on two-way streets, each solved to proven optimality. The harder problems below are solved exactly only at smaller sizes.
92
benchmark networks from the research literature. 53 of them are real roads in the UK and Belgium.
4
kinds of network. Two-way, one-way, mixed, and a different cost for each direction (the windy postman).
2
kinds of answer. Exact where the problem and the time allow. Otherwise a heuristic, a quick method that comes with a lower bound showing how far it can be from the optimum.

Nearly thirty years later

The method of 1997 is unchanged in outline. Each stage now uses what is available today, and the paths the project left open are followed up. Proved marks what holds on every network that meets its assumptions, and Measured what was observed on the networks tested.

Larger networks

1997 Exact up to about 20 odd junctions on a 150 MHz Pentium, then a time-limited search.

2026 Proved Two-way and one-way streets are solved exactly in polynomial time. Measured About 12,000 odd junctions in a median of 110 seconds.

More kinds of street

1997 Two-way streets. One-way streets proposed as future work.

2026 Proved Win's method for the windy postman is never above twice the optimum. Measured It averages 0.10% above 99 published optima, with a worst case of 0.44%. Mixed towns are solved exactly up to 400 junctions.

Only some streets, several postmen

1997 Proposed a dearer first pass, and noted that a shift limits the work.

2026 Proved On two-way networks, never above 3/2 of the optimum with some streets, and 2 − 1/k with k postmen when one optimal route is split. No such factor is proved on the other networks. Measured Means of 0.62% above 47 published optima with some streets and 2.15% above 629 with several postmen, with worst cases of 7.6% and 17.2%, on windy networks. Vehicles with a capacity: a median 5.8% above the best published costs, with the number of vehicles left free.

Exact, heuristic and certified

1997 Branch-and-bound, exact when it finished in time.

2026 Matching and flow where the problem is polynomial, branch-and-cut where it is not, and heuristics that report their distance from a lower bound. Each large matching carries a certificate that is checked on every run.

Open and in the browser

1997 Turbo C on MS-DOS.

2026 Open-source Python, and a solver in the browser for street networks of up to 2,500 junctions.
